Vehicle dynamics · phone sensors · Nov 2026

I rebuilt a metro tunnel in 3D from the sensors in my phone.

Nobody publishes what the inside of a metro tunnel looks like — where it climbs, where it bends, where the train has to fight gravity to stop. So I measured it. A phone lying on the seat records which way it turns, how the air pressure changes and how hard it's pushed. Put those together and you get the tunnel: 12 km of it, nine stations, every hill and curve — and with it, exactly where the brakes do the most work.

Method

How a phone sees a tunnel.

BendsGyroscope

It measures how fast the phone rotates. The part around the vertical is the train turning. Multiply by speed, add it up, and you get the tunnel's path seen from above. The gyroscope's own drift is measured while the train stands in a station — where it can't be turning — and taken out.

HeightBarometer

Air pressure drops about 0.12 hPa for every metre you climb. I read it only while the train stands at a platform: while it moves, the air it pushes through the tunnel swings the pressure by more than the climb itself. Between stations the height is drawn as a straight line — interpolated, not measured.

SpeedAccelerometer

Integrating acceleration gives speed, but a tilt of half a degree adds up to metres per second within a minute. Two facts fix it: the train is stopped at both ends of each run, and the distance between stations is known. A run that needs a big correction to meet both is thrown out.

Brakes

Where the brakes work hardest — and where gravity helps.

To stop, the brakes have to take away the train's motion energy, ½v², plus any height it loses on the way down, g·Δh — per kilogram of train. On flat track that's the motion alone. On a slope, the tunnel decides.

motion energy when slowing starts height lost while slowing (downhill) height gained while slowing (uphill — gravity brakes for free)

S3 → S4 · 2.7% downhill534 J/kg

The train starts slowing at 88 km/h while still running downhill. Its motion energy is 300 J/kg; the 24 m it drops on the way adds another 234. The brakes absorb nearly twice what flat track would ask of them — every tonne of train sheds over half a megajoule.

S2 → S3 · 2.9% uphill115 J/kg

Here the train starts slowing at 75 km/h, but it is climbing: 10.5 m of rise takes 103 J/kg off its speed for free. The brakes only handle about half the motion energy — roughly a fifth of what the downhill stop costs them.

These are upper bounds for the brakes: rolling resistance and air drag also take a share while slowing. It is also the energy a regenerative drive could try to win back — how much it actually recovers depends on the drive and on whether another train on the line can use it at that moment.

Pull and slow are the peaks of the speed-derived acceleration (smoothed to 0.3 Hz). Jerk is how abruptly the push changes. Vibration is the ISO 2631-1 frequency-weighted value; a phone on a seat reads differently from a seat-mounted sensor, so compare these with each other, not with published ISO limits.

Checks

How I know it isn't made up.

The phone never saw a map. So the map is the test: how much the track really turns between stations (OpenStreetMap), against how much the gyroscope says it turned. And where a stretch was ridden more than once, how much its height change moved between rides.

Limits

What this can't tell you.

  • How deep the tunnel is. The barometer gives height changes, not depth below ground.
  • The shape of the slope between stations. Moving air hides it; the straight lines between stations are drawn, not measured.
  • A precise plan view. It follows the mapped track to within 30–130 m at worst; good enough to see every curve, not to survey one.
  • Statistics. Most stretches are drawn from one clean run. This is a case study of what a phone can measure, not an average of the line.

Privacy

Why the stations have no names.

These are rides I actually took. Named stations and dates would add up to a record of where I go, so the page keeps only the measurements: stations are numbered, the view isn't tied to north, and nothing here says when or how often a stretch was ridden.

Where it came from

A by-product of an app I've stopped building.

This started inside WhereisMyRide, an app I built to measure Istanbul's rail system with a phone — Tekir the cat was its mascot. I've stopped developing the app; plenty of good transit apps already exist. The measurements were the part worth keeping.
